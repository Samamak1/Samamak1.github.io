Vibration sensing and data acquisition

A two-sensor instrumentation project, three channels captured in LabVIEW and 1,022 samples analyzed to separate genuine events from mounting and wiring faults.

Where
Wichita State University, ME 633
When
Spring 2016
My role
Individual project, fixture, wiring, acquisition, and analysis
Tools
LabVIEW DAQ, Arduino Uno, Keyestudio KS0037, MiniSense 100, Excel

The idea

Detect surface vibration with two sensor types, record the amplitudes and periods, convert them into an earthquake style magnitude, and compare that magnitude against a criticality threshold so a user gets a warning with time to act. The Keyestudio KS0037 gave an analog and a digital channel and responded to small movements. The MiniSense 100 piezo element was there for the large events and to cross check the noisier channel.

The setup

I designed and assembled the sensor mounts, the test fixture, and the physical setup, then wired the sensors to the Arduino Uno and brought the signals into LabVIEW as three analog input channels. AI0 carried the Keyestudio analog output, AI1 the MiniSense, and AI2 the Keyestudio digital output. LabVIEW wrote every sample to a spreadsheet so the run could be plotted and compared afterward.

Every run followed the same loop. Excite the surface, capture the channels, plot them, and check whether a spike on one channel showed up on the others. When a channel disagreed, I swapped channels, reseated connections, and repeated the trial until the cause was clear.

  • Three channels sampled together, so timing could be compared across sensors.
  • 1,022 samples preserved in the final data set, sample numbers 4310 to 5331.
  • Peak amplitudes of 3.57 V on the Keyestudio analog channel, 4.99 V on the MiniSense, and 4.17 V on the digital channel.
  • Channel swaps, connection checks, and repeat trials used to separate sensor faults from mounting, cabling, and mechanical effects.

The data

What the two sensors showed side by side is the useful part. The analog channel is sensitive but slow to settle, so it smears events together. The MiniSense is less sensitive to small motion but returns to baseline quickly, which makes it the better event counter. Reading them together gave a cleaner picture than either one alone, and it made the bad readings stand out, because a spike that appeared on only one channel usually traced back to a loose lead or a mount that had shifted rather than to the surface.

From amplitude to magnitude

The writeup used the standard body wave and surface wave magnitude correlations, where the amplitude of ground motion in microns and the corresponding period in seconds feed a magnitude estimate with a distance and depth correction. The recorded amplitudes were the input to that calculation, and the criticality comparison was the planned next step for turning the rig into a warning device.

The magnitude calculation was outlined in the project writeup and was not calibrated against a seismograph, so this page presents the rig, the acquisition, and the data, not a validated seismic instrument.
