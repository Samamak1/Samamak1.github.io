Kite buggy design and structural analysis

A five-person design project that sized a 29.7 pound aluminum kite buggy against a ten foot drop, a 30 pound weight limit, and a $400 budget. My share was the load, stress, and deflection analysis.

Where
Wichita State University, ME 439 Mechanical Engineering Design I
When
Spring 2015
My role
Load, stress, and deflection calculations, shared with two teammates
Team
Five students, CATIA modeling and drafting by a teammate

The brief

Design a kite buggy that beats the market on price, weight, portability, and safety. Manufacturing cost under $400, weight at or under 30 lb, folds into the trunk of a small car, survives a 10 foot drop, and stays controllable at speeds up to 40 mph. The team split the work at the first meeting. One teammate owned the CATIA model and drafting, one owned the report, and three of us, including me, owned the calculations.

The worst case

The governing load came from a scenario where an updraft lifts the rider ten feet and the line breaks. With a 30 lb cart, a 180 lb rider, a 0.25 second impact, and 6 inches of spring travel in the seat, the impulse analysis gave about 94 lb from the cart and 3,600 lb from the rider, 3,694 lb on the frame. Every tube diameter and wall thickness in the design traces back to that number.

Tipping before bending

Before the stress work we asked whether the cart would tip. The free body diagrams below carry the kite pull at an angle, the rider's ability to shift weight, and the pull height. The moment balance alone says stability improves with a wider axle, a lower pull height, and a rear set center of mass, but it also blows up as the upward pull approaches the total weight. Adding friction closed the gap. With a tire on dry asphalt at a friction coefficient of 0.7, the cart slips before it tips, and tipping only shows up when the tire digs in and the effective coefficient spikes. The design rule that came out of it was simple. Make the axle as long as the mass and cost limits allow.

Sizing the frame

Aluminum, steel, and titanium were run through the same loop, assume an axle length, compute the moment, back out a tube diameter, then check mass and cost per foot against the limits. Aluminum 6061 T451 met both, at 2 inch outside diameter with a 0.25 inch wall. The rear axle finished at 28 inches, the cart at 36 inches. Under the worst case load the rear axle sees about 1,850 lb of shear, 9,330 in lb of bending moment, 0.061 inches of deflection, and 17,378 psi against a 21,000 psi yield, a design factor of 1.21. Under normal riding loads the same axle runs at a factor of 20.3, and the side rails at 21.7. The team accepted the thin margin on the drop case because it was taken to an extreme and the sprung seat exists to protect the rider in exactly that event.

The design
